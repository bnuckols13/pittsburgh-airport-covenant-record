# Pittsburgh built a $1.7 billion airport terminal. What it costs airlines to fly there is the highest since the pandemic year.

*The new terminal was financed on two promises: that the airport would pay for it out of what it earns, and that flying out of Pittsburgh would get cheaper. Documents filed with the airport's lenders show its own consultant expecting neither. On the money actually pledged to those lenders, the airport comes in under the 25 percent cushion it promised in five of the next six years, and what it charges airlines to board a passenger is higher in every year of the forecast than it is today. The contract that puts the carriers on the hook for the difference expires at the end of 2028.*

**By Lena Rose Williams** | [Outlet] | Aug. 30, 2026

---

On a Saturday morning in October, with the federal government shut down, the state budget months overdue and several hundred invited guests seated under 811,000 square feet of new glass and steel, the county executive stood beside the chief executive of the airport authority and called the terminal a catalyst for the community's economic jet engine; Reps. Chris Deluzio, Summer Lee and Mike Kelly and Sen. Dave McCormick were there to hear it, five and a half weeks before the building took its first passenger, and by the time the doors opened to the public at noon nobody on the dais had said what it would cost to carry a terminal that had come in eight years and two months after the board approved it at $1.1 billion.

Sara Innamorato, the county executive, cited the Authority's commissioned economic-impact study, which projects $2.5 billion in economic impact and 14,300 jobs created during construction [TK: name the consultant who produced it]. Christina Cassotis, who has run the Allegheny County Airport Authority since 2015 and whose contract the board extended in January through 2030, called it an airport built for Pittsburgh, by Pittsburgh. The airlines that will pay for the building had signed the agreement that made it possible and had sent no one to speak.

The number the board approved in September 2017 was $1.1 billion. At the groundbreaking four years later it was about $1.39 billion. The bond statement the Authority sold in 2023 put it at $1,569,362,914, and the one it sold in April 2025 put it at $1,702,562,914. Each increase was approved by the board and by the airlines, whose agreements and subsequent votes cover up to $1.7 billion in terminal and airside project costs.

An airline does not buy a ticket at Pittsburgh; it rents counter space, pays to land, and pays to use the gates. Divide what an airline owes the airport in a year by the passengers it puts on planes there and you get the figure the industry watches, and on the Authority's most recent federal filing that figure was $16.10 in fiscal 2025. The Authority told the Post-Gazette in January that it expects $17.64 this year. The only higher figure in the record is 2020, when the pandemic emptied the airport and the charge was computed against almost no passengers.

On the Authority's own calculation, which is a different series from the federal filing and is never subtracted against it, a single narrow-body jet leaving Pittsburgh with 175 people aboard carried about $1,700 in airport charges in 2019. On the same basis the consultant's forecast for this year works out to about $3,350, and for 2030 to about $3,590. The multiplication is [Outlet]'s.

In 2019, the year the airport finished paying for the terminal it was about to replace, it made $21,190,890 in debt service payments and then owed nothing on a terminal for four years while interest on the new borrowing was capitalized.

Cassotis said in 2017 that the charge would fall from $12.69 to $9.73 by 2023, in 2017 dollars. The 2023 figure the Authority's management discussion reports is $11.34, and Table IV of the same statement reports $11.50, a conflict the document does not resolve. Her chief financial officer said at the 2021 groundbreaking that it would settle between $10 and $11. The Authority's own consultant now expects $20.53 by 2030.

From 2022 on, every year came in above what the Authority had forecast, and the miss grew each year: 79 cents in 2022, $1.61 in 2023, $1.73 in 2024. The subtraction is [Outlet]'s.

Pittsburgh boarded 4,964,361 passengers in 2024, beating the strong-recovery case in the Authority's own 2021 forecast by 40,361 and clearing the weak one by nearly 600,000.

Cassotis told the Post-Gazette in January that the cost per enplaned passenger would have risen regardless of the new terminal, because the old landside building would have needed substantial renovations to keep operating.

The airport promised its lenders that every year it would earn 25 percent more than it owes them. Counting only the money it actually pledged to those lenders, its own consultant expects it to come in under that line in five of the next six years. A reserve the Authority tops up whenever it chooses makes up the difference in the forecast, and no contract obliges it to keep topping it up. The airline agreement that covers the shortfall ends on Dec. 31, 2028.

## Airlines paid $16.10 a passenger at Pittsburgh in fiscal 2025, more than at 16 of the largest hubs

When the airport needs more money than it earns, the airlines are billed for the difference under their contracts.

Carriers are not charged by the head. They pay rent and landing fees, $298.36 per square foot of terminal space and $4.06 per 1,000 pounds of landing weight as of January. The per-passenger figure is that total divided by boardings, and the industry calls it cost per enplaned passenger. It is not a ticket fee charged to passengers, Bob Kerlik, the Authority's spokesman, wrote in 2019, but the total of all airport costs to airlines divided by the carriers' departing passengers. He wrote it in Blue Sky News, which the Authority publishes and Kerlik edits. [TK: capture the page.]

Two different calculations go by that name and this story keeps them apart throughout. The Authority computes one under its cost-sharing agreement with the airlines and prints it in its bond statements and annual reports. The other comes off Form 5100-127, which every commercial airport files with the Federal Aviation Administration each year. The $16.10 is the federal figure for fiscal 2025.

On that basis Pittsburgh is the fifth most expensive of 32 medium hubs, and costs an airline more per boarded passenger than Atlanta, Charlotte, Denver, Dallas-Fort Worth, Detroit, Minneapolis, Phoenix, Las Vegas, Orlando, Tampa, Nashville, Baltimore, Dulles, Reagan National, Fort Lauderdale or Houston Intercontinental. Atlanta boards 53 million passengers a year at $4.48. Not every large hub is cheaper: Salt Lake City reported $17.88 for the same year.

> **[PLATE FC — rank.** *Pittsburgh now costs an airline more than Atlanta.* The 16 large hubs it exceeds, with Pittsburgh's medium-hub rank stated on the plate. C-grade, compiled from Form 127 filings; pull Atlanta, Denver and Dallas-Fort Worth before print.**]**

Kansas City opened a comparable single terminal in February 2023, into a somewhat larger market with no connecting carrier feeding it; it boarded 5,915,078 passengers in 2024 on the FAA's count, which is the basis on which both airports are comparable and which puts Pittsburgh at 4,862,376 rather than the 4,964,361 its own statement reports. Kansas City's charge was $15.37 in fiscal 2025, and the forecast in its 2020 bond statement peaks at $17.40 in 2026 and comes back down to $16.68 by 2027. Pittsburgh's April 2025 forecast runs above $19 in every year from 2026 on and reaches $20.53 in 2030. The two forecasts were written five years apart, one before the terminal it describes opened and one after.

> **[PLATE FB — two lines, 2025 to 2030.** *Kansas City built the same thing and is coming back down.* Same project, opposite shape. Kansas City figures are C-grade; pull its Official Statement before this runs.**]**

KBRA, which rated the 2025 bonds A+, listed among the credit challenges that a rising cost per enplaned passenger could discourage service expansion or retention, particularly among low-cost and ultra-low-cost carriers. Those carriers flew 29.7 percent of Pittsburgh's departing passengers in 2014 and 44.3 percent in 2024, on [Outlet]'s count from the Authority's carrier tables [TK: name which carriers are counted as low-cost; the classification decides the result]. Seth Lehman, a senior director at Fitch Ratings, told the Bond Buyer in April 2025 that the borrowing would push Pittsburgh's cost above what is standard for an A-rated airport.

Spirit Airlines, which carried about 3.5 percent of Pittsburgh's traffic when it went, ceased operating everywhere on May 2, 2026. Pittsburgh's traffic in the first half of this year ran 1.1 percent behind last year's, which the airport attributes to Spirit and to fuel prices.

How much of an airport's cost reaches a fare is contested. Studies of per-passenger taxes added at booking find carriers passing nearly all of it into ticket prices. Studies of operating costs find less. Koopmans and Lieshout, writing in the Journal of Air Transport Management in 2016, put the share passed into fares below half when a cost increase hits a single carrier and above half when it hits every carrier in a market at once. None of that work examines Pittsburgh.

Passengers pay the airport directly as well. A $4.50 charge sits on every eligible ticket and did not come down when the terminal opened, which it could not have: the federal cap is $4.50 for nearly every airport in the country. Rental customers have paid $8 a day since Jan. 1, 2025. Pennsylvania taxpayers pay through a $12.4 million appropriation of gambling money that goes to the airport instead of somewhere else. And parking went from five products to three when the terminal opened, according to the Authority's own rate schedule, with advance and drive-up rates split and the cheapest option within walking distance of the terminal ended.

## In five of six forecast years, pledged revenue alone falls under the 125 percent the Authority promised

The airport made its lenders a promise it has to keep every year: after paying what it costs to run the place, it will hold 25 percent more than it owes them. The bond documents call that the rate covenant and print the number as 1.25. A reserve account counts toward the total alongside the airport's earnings, and the statements print only the two added together.

Dividing the consultant's own rows shows what the pledged revenue does by itself. In the April 2025 forecast it reaches 1.35 in 2025 and then runs 1.12, 1.13, 1.12, 1.18 and 1.19 through 2030, under 1.25 in five of six years. In the 2021 forecast, which runs four years under two recovery cases, pledged revenue alone falls under 1.25 in two of the four base-case years and in all four of the slow-recovery years. The two documents are two bets and their years are not added together. The division is [Outlet]'s; the statements print only the combined ratio.

Recomputing the combined column from the same rows reproduces every printed ratio to within rounding, which is the check that the reading is right. Vintages shown side by side and never summed, and recovery cases inside a vintage never summed either.**]**

That reserve is the Coverage Account. It sits ninth in line as money moves through the airport's accounts, and the indenture funds it, in the document's own words, at the discretion of the Authority, on or before the tenth business day of each month. It is capped at 25 percent of the year's debt service. The 2025 forecast assumes that ceiling in five of its six years, and the 2021 forecast assumes it in every scenario-year but one.

Gas and casino revenue count toward the promise only where the Authority chooses to designate them, which the documents call designating Other Pledged Revenues, and its own exhibit shows it designating none of either in any year from 2020 through 2024. What it designated instead was federal pandemic relief: $13.1 million in 2020, $19.1 million in 2021, $13.1 million in 2022, and either $3.0 million or $4.0 million in 2023, depending on which page of the same statement is read. Nothing at all in 2024. Gaming money returns to the pledge in 2025, at $8.2 million, under the commitment the airlines voted in January of that year.

> **[PLATE V / F3 — designated revenue against the gap.** *What the Authority designated, and what it was.* Two panels, one dollar scale, adjacent and not joined, because no document states that this designated revenue is the source that closes this particular gap. Relabel the series as Other Pledged Revenues by component, from Exhibit E. The gap the 2025 forecast leaves runs $5.6 million to $11.7 million a year, against $0.5 million to $5.1 million in the 2021 forecast.**]**

Two cautions belong in the same breath as those figures. Footnote 2 of the same statement says the coverage calculation was not applicable from 2020 through 2023 because interest was capitalized and no debt service was due, so those four years tested nothing and there is no track record either way. And the Coverage Account contribution the 2025 statement prints for 2026 is $21.2 million, which matches neither the designated series nor the $11.575 million the airlines voted. Three different amounts are in play, and the story cannot merge them.

Neither source behind the commitment is fixed. Gaming money has been flat at $12.4 million a year since 2020, under a statute the Authority's own filing says could be amended to reduce or eliminate payments. Gas royalties ran between $5.56 million and $12.21 million across 2019 through 2024, and in May 2022 the Authority amended its CNX lease to permit deductions from gross revenue. The Authority says CNX committed in exchange to increase drilling and add wells, under a seven-year commitment carrying penalties, and that the deductions apply only to Marcellus wells begun after the amendment.

Missing the covenant in a single year is not a default. The indenture requires the Authority to hire a consultant, take its advice and raise its rates. Only a second consecutive miss, after rates have gone up, is an Event of Default.

## Three supports end Dec. 31, 2028. The bonds run to 2056.

The Airline Operating Agreements took effect Jan. 1, 2020 and expire Dec. 31, 2028, extendable three years only by mutual agreement. The Authority's statement lists eleven signatory carriers holding about 97.8 percent of the airport's 2024 market share in one place and ten in another, and one of the eleven, Spirit, no longer exists [VERIFY: that Spirit appears on the signatory list itself, not only in the market-share table; if it does not, cut this clause]. That contract is what makes the carriers responsible for whatever the airport fails to earn.

The carriers also hold a vote. Weighted by how much of the airport each one uses, a majority can bind them all, and the agreements call it a majority-in-interest vote. In January 2025 they used it to commit no less than $8.8 million of gaming or gas money for 2025 and $11.575 million a year for 2026 through 2028, designated into the pledge. The same vote waived the $5 million annual capital-fund deposit for those same three years. Both end with the agreement.

The Authority borrowed about $1.66 billion across three bond sales, in August 2021, October 2023 and April 2025, and its combined schedule totals about $3.23 billion in principal and interest by the time the last of it matures in 2056. Twenty-eight of those years fall after the date on which every arrangement holding the current arithmetic together expires. The consultant's forecast stops in 2030, two years past all of it, at $20.53 a passenger.

## The Authority has never released the comparison behind the $1.7 billion decision

In September 2017 the board voted to build rather than renovate. The Post-Gazette reported that Cassotis said the Authority had considered other approaches, including renovations and relocating the boarding terminal, and that all proved more costly than replacing the landside building.

Federal guidance for airport master plans requires that comparison, weighing each option against a wide range of evaluation criteria, including operational, environmental and financial impacts. The Authority's 2017 annual report says the plan is on file with the Federal Aviation Administration. Neither the Authority nor the FAA has released it, and no estimate for renovating the old terminal has appeared in the nine years since.

The savings that justified the project were the maintenance the Authority would stop paying once it closed the old terminal. The Allegheny Institute, a conservative research group in Pittsburgh, went at that arithmetic three weeks after the vote, setting the Authority's own estimate of roughly $23 million a year in avoided upkeep against roughly $75 million a year in debt service, a figure the Institute derived by assuming 30 years at 5.5 percent on what was then a $1.1 billion project rather than reading a payment schedule. The project is $1.7 billion now.

The Authority did produce a method. Its consultant broke the savings into labor, contract and lifecycle components, forecast each by type and year with the Authority's operations management, and phased them to full realization by 2030. The same passage discloses what the phasing does not cover: the Authority estimates it will incur $2,047,000 a year in additional operating and maintenance expense because it did not demolish the old terminal, and it netted that cost against the savings rather than reporting it separately.

The old building is closed and still standing. The line for demolishing it read $33 million in the bond statement the Authority sold in 2023 and $0 in the one it sold in April 2025. The statement says the Authority postponed the demolition to reduce project costs and associated debt, with the signatory airlines' agreement.

The plan cut the airport from 75 gates to 51. Pittsburgh boarded 4,964,361 passengers in 2024, fewer than it boarded in any year before US Airways began dismantling its connecting hub in 2004. Eighty-four cents of every dollar that paid for the building is bond proceeds and premium. Between four and a half and five cents is federal grant money, a range rather than a figure because the Authority reports federal Airport Improvement Program money and Pennsylvania state grants on one line and does not separate them. The rest comes from charges on passengers and rental customers and from the Authority's own funds. [Outlet]'s arithmetic on the Authority's own sources-and-uses table.

Total passenger traffic tells a different story than boardings do. The Authority counted 9.95 million arriving and departing passengers in 2024, which it says exceeded pre-pandemic 2019 by 2 percent and was the highest total the airport had seen in nearly 20 years.

The question has a history. In February 2018, five months after the board approved the project, John Fiorita, a former US Airways plant maintenance and facilities director who had worked in Pittsburgh, asked the Post-Gazette whether the claimed savings had counted the maintenance still owed on the buildings the Authority was keeping. "Have any of those numbers been taken into account?" he said. In the same account a resident named David Allinder asked Allegheny County Council to hold a public hearing. The Authority said it had held three public meetings, two of them public workshops, and had run a website for public comment.

Council did not hold a hearing. Its president said the county had no jurisdiction over the Authority. No elected body in Allegheny County has taken the question up since. Council does confirm every member of the airport board, and in July it seated state Sen. Devlin Robinson, R-37th, on a 12-3 vote [TK: confirm the date against council minutes; the account is dated July 8, 2026 and places the vote the previous day]. Bethany Hallam, Lissa Geiger Shulman and Alex Rose voted no. The two other nominees passed on a voice vote. None of the three has explained the vote publicly. David Minnotte, who chaired the board through the 2017 approval, left in April 2024 after 20 years. In January the board voted unanimously to extend Cassotis's contract through Jan. 31, 2030, past the year the airline contracts expire.

Tasso Katselas designed the midfield terminal, part of which the new building replaces and part of which, the airside he drew, is still in service. He told the Post-Gazette weeks before the first bonds were sold: "There's no way anyone can justify it that has any sense."

Three things expire on Dec. 31, 2028. The bonds run to 2056.
