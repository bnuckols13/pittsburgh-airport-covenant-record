# THE COVENANT — what 1.25 is, and why it matters

## Part 1 — Eight questions, in the order a person actually asks them

### 1. What is a covenant?

A promise written into the contract between the Authority and the people who lent it money. Not a law. Not a regulator's rule. Nobody at the FAA or in Harrisburg enforces it. The bondholders' trustee, Bank of New York Mellon, receives an annual certificate saying whether it was kept.

That matters for the story, because it means the covenant is not a public-interest safeguard. **It is a protection the lenders bought.**

### 2. Why do these bonds need one at all?

Because nothing else stands behind them.

These are revenue bonds. The Authority has no power to tax. Allegheny County has not guaranteed them, and neither has Pennsylvania. If the airport stops earning, there is no other pocket. The Official Statement says so directly, and it also tells investors there is no acceleration and no involuntary bankruptcy: nobody can call the loan in, and nobody gets the building.

So the lenders' entire protection is the airport's own income. The covenant is how they made a claim on it in advance.

### 3. What does the promise actually say?

Here is the part that is usually explained wrong, including in this case's own drafts.

**It is not a promise to earn a certain amount. It is a promise to charge enough.** That is why the document calls it the *rate* covenant. The Authority commits to setting its landing fees, terminal rents and other charges at a level that produces the required coverage.

Read it as an obligation to price, not an obligation to perform. Everything else follows from that.

### 4. Why 1.25 and not 1.00?

If you promised your lender you would earn exactly your annual payment, then any bad year is a missed payment. There is no room.

So lenders ask for a margin. **1.25 means: for every dollar of debt payment due this year, show a dollar and twenty-five cents.** The extra twenty-five cents is the room for a soft year.

1.25 is the ordinary figure in the airport bond market rather than anything specific to Pittsburgh. **Verify that against a named primary before printing it**, because "standard" is a claim and this case has no source for it yet beyond a finding aid.

### 5. A dollar and twenty-five cents of what?

Not everything the airport takes in.

**Revenues, minus the cost of running the airport, equals Net Revenues.** Salaries, maintenance, utilities, snow removal, police, all of it comes out first. What remains is what the test is run on, and it is also what the debt is paid from.

And only *pledged* revenue counts. Gaming money and gas royalties sit outside the definition of Revenues in the indenture, except to the extent the Authority chooses to designate them in each year. That is the seam the whole investigation runs through.

### 6. So where does the Coverage Account come in?

This is the part worth slowing down on, because it is the finding.

The covenant does not say Net Revenues must reach 1.25 times debt service. It says **Net Revenues together with the amount on deposit in the Coverage Account** must reach it.

The Coverage Account is a second pot. Three facts about it, all from the indenture:

* It sits **ninth** in line when money comes into the airport, behind the debt payment itself, behind reserves, behind maintenance and renewal.
* Money goes into it **"at the discretion of the Authority."** There is no obligation to fund it.
* What can be counted from it is **capped at 25 percent** of that year's debt payment.

Now put the cap next to the target.

**Twenty-five percent is exactly the distance from 1.00 to 1.25.**

Which means a fully funded Coverage Account can supply the entire margin by itself. An airport earning exactly its debt payment and not one cent more still passes the test, provided it chooses to move discretionary money into that account. **The cushion does not have to be earned. It can be deposited.**

### 7. What happens if the number comes up short?

Much less than people assume, and this is where the story turns.

A miss is not a default. The indenture says the Authority must hire a consultant, adopt its recommendations, and **"take all lawful measures to revise the schedule of rates, tolls, fees, rentals and charges."** One miss, cured by raising prices, is not an Event of Default. Only a second consecutive miss, after the rates have already gone up, is.

And even then there is no acceleration, no lien on the terminal, and no involuntary bankruptcy. The Authority could keep collecting money and running the airport indefinitely.

### 8. So who ends up paying?

The airlines, first, by contract. Pittsburgh sets airline rates on a cost-centre residual basis: every other source of money is counted first and the airlines are billed the remainder, in whatever amount the Authority needs for operating costs, debt service, deposits and this covenant.

**Which gives the sentence this whole explainer exists to produce:**

> The rate covenant is not a test the airport can fail. It is a machine that turns a revenue shortfall into a price increase.

It does not protect the airport. It protects the bondholders, by obliging the airport to charge more. That is the connection between a line in a bond document and what it costs to fly out of Pittsburgh, and it is the bridge into station 3.

## Part 2 — The arithmetic, so you can run it yourself

The whole test is one division:

```
                Net Revenues  +  Coverage Account deposit
   coverage  =  ----------------------------------------
                    Aggregate Annual Debt Service
```

**2026, from the consultant's April 8, 2025 table.** Dollars in thousands.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| Aggregate Annual Debt Service | $84,670 | what is owed |
| To clear 1.25, the airport needs | **$105,838** | 1.25 × 84,670 |
| Forecast Net Revenues | $94,616 | what it expects to earn |
| Short by | **$11,222** | |
| Coverage Account deposit | $21,168 | exactly 25% of 84,670, the contractual maximum |
| | | |
| Ratio on Net Revenues alone | **1.118** | 94,616 ÷ 84,670 |
| Ratio as the document prints it | **1.37** | (94,616 + 21,168) ÷ 84,670 |

Two things to notice.

**The deposit is more than twice the shortfall.** The airport is $11.2 million short of the target and puts in $21.2 million, because it counts the maximum rather than the amount needed. That is why the printed number is 1.37 and not 1.25.

**Only the division is ours.** Both of the top-line numbers are the consultant's own printed rows. The document prints only the combined figure. Say so in the same sentence, every time.

## Part 3 — Analogies, ranked, with the trap in each

**The one already in the case, and it is the best.** From `FINDINGS-2026-08-29.md`:

> A lender asks you to show income of $1.25 for every dollar of mortgage payment. Your salary covers $1.12. You make up the difference with help from a relative, who is permitted to count under the lender's own rules and who has committed only through 2028. The mortgage runs to 2056. You are breaking no rule. Your salary still does not cover the test.

It carries the discretion, the permission, the term mismatch and the innocence, in five sentences. **Trap:** a relative is a third party, and the Coverage Account is the Authority's own money moved from one place to another. If a reader takes the relative literally they will think somebody else is paying. Fix it in the wording: money the airport is allowed to set aside for itself, out of income it never promised the lender.

**A second, for the pricing point.** A landlord promises the bank that rents will always cover the mortgage a quarter over. If the building empties out, the promise does not forgive the landlord. It obliges them to raise the rent on whoever is still there. **Trap:** do not run this one far enough to imply the airport can raise prices without limit. It cannot; 2028 and the majority-in-interest vote are the limit.

**One to avoid.** Do not use a credit score, a stress test or a bank capital ratio. Every one of them implies a regulator, and there is no regulator here.

## Part 4 — Is Pittsburgh unusual? Probably not, and that changes the story

A search on Aug. 29 turned up a review of 57 United States airport bond documents finding that **59 percent use "soft" rate covenants**, meaning ones that let a coverage or rolling account count toward the test the way Pittsburgh's does. **That came through a finding aid rather than a source, and it needs a primary before it is printed.** If it holds, it matters a great deal, and in two directions.

**It weakens any framing built on novelty.** Nobody did anything exotic. The structure is ordinary and it is disclosed.

**And it makes the story better, not worse.** The finding was never that the Authority broke a rule. It is that **a published number does not mean what a reader assumes it means**, and if that is true across most of the market then Pittsburgh is an example rather than an outlier. The piece should say so. Acknowledging it is also the strongest available protection against the response that this is how airport finance works.

**The thing that is not ordinary is the reliance.** Five of six years in the newest forecast, five of six assuming the account at its cap, with a gap running $5.59 million to $11.72 million a year, against money committed only through 2028 and a debt running to 2056. Ordinariness of structure is not ordinariness of dependence. **That distinction is the one to hold on to**, and it is what the right of reply should be built around.

## Part 5 — The sidebar, draft

*About 350 words. Publishable alongside the piece. Pittsburgh Water: no reporter, no adjectives, one idea per sentence.*

---

**What a coverage covenant is**

When the Allegheny County Airport Authority borrowed to build the terminal, it made bond buyers a promise about money.

The promise is unusual in one respect. It is not a promise to earn a particular amount. It is a promise to charge enough. The Authority agreed to set its landing fees and terminal rents at a level that leaves it, after paying the cost of running the airport, with at least $1.25 for every $1.00 it owes on the bonds that year. The documents call it the rate covenant.

The extra 25 cents is the lenders' margin for a bad year. It is the ordinary arrangement in airport borrowing.

The covenant matters because nothing else stands behind these bonds. The Authority cannot levy a tax. Neither Allegheny County nor the Commonwealth has guaranteed the debt. If the airport does not earn the money, there is no other source.

What the Authority is allowed to count toward the test is set out in the trust indenture: airport revenue after operating costs, **plus** whatever sits in an account called the Coverage Account.

That account is a second pot. It sits ninth in line when money arrives at the airport. Deposits into it are made, the indenture says, "at the discretion of the Authority." And the most that can ever be counted from it is 25 percent of the year's debt payment.

Twenty-five percent is the whole margin. An airport earning exactly its debt payment can still pass the test, if it chooses to put the maximum into that account.

Coming up short does not mean default. The indenture gives the Authority a year to fix it: hire a consultant, take the advice, raise the rates. Only a second consecutive miss, after prices have gone up, counts as a default. Even then, no one can call in the loan and no one can take the terminal.

Which is why the covenant is not really a safety test. Under the agreement Pittsburgh uses, airlines are billed whatever is left over after every other source is counted. **A shortfall does not arrive as a missed payment. It arrives as a higher price.**

## Part 6 — Rules carried in

The do-not-write list in [`FINDINGS-2026-08-29.md`](FINDINGS-2026-08-29.md) §A governs everything here. Restated because this file will be read on its own:

* **Never default, breach or violation.** Nothing has happened. The forecast complies with the covenant as the indenture writes it.
* **Never "the covenant fails" or "the test the plan does not pass."** The accurate claim is the ratio on Net Revenues alone, in those words. **`covenant-plates.html` currently carries the heading "Take away the discretionary account and the covenant fails," which breaks this rule and needs changing before that file goes anywhere.**
* **The division is ours and is labelled in the same sentence.** The document prints only the combined figure.
* **Do not say the Coverage Account was never funded.** Footnote 2 says the calculation was not applicable for 2020 through 2023 because no debt service was due. There is no track record either way.
* **Do not mix the 2021 and 2025 tables.** Two forecasts, not one series.
* **Say "coverage" only after saying what it measures.** Never open on the word.

## Part 7 — What to verify before any of this prints

1. **That 1.25 is the market standard**, against a named primary rather than an assertion. A rating agency methodology or a bond-market reference, not a dashboard.
2. **The 59 percent figure** on soft covenants across 57 airport documents, which currently comes through a finding aid.
3. **The indenture text itself.** Appendix C is a form. The executed Master Trust Indenture is still a named empty cell, and every quotation about the flow of funds, the discretion and the 25 percent cap rests on the form until it lands.
4. **The compliance certificates.** They would show what was actually deposited, as against what the forecast assumes. They are item 3 of the revised Right-to-Know and the single most important ask on H3.
5. **Whether the Coverage Account is funded from operations**, which is H3's disproof path and needs the 2025 audited fund statements.
