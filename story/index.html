The story

What pays for Pittsburgh’s new terminal

Pittsburgh International Airport opened its new landside terminal in November 2025, financed by three bond issues in 2021, 2023 and 2025.

The Allegheny County Airport Authority covenanted to set its rates and charges so that its net revenue, together with the amount on deposit in a reserve called the Coverage Account, is at least 1.25 times its annual debt service. Its own consultant forecasts six years that comply. Documents analyzed by this reporting show what the compliance is made of.

What the coverage figure is made of

The Official Statement for the 2025 bonds prints one coverage figure for each forecast year, and every one of them clears 1.25. The printed figures are 1.60, 1.37, 1.36, 1.37, 1.43 and 1.44.

A footnote on that table reads: “Includes Other Pledged Revenues.” The Master Trust Indenture defines those as moneys “not constituting Revenues” that the Authority designates for a period. Exhibit E of the same document gives the amount for every year.

Subtracting the one from the other is this reporting’s arithmetic. The Authority prints only the combined figure. Sources: PDF 202 (printed B-16) and PDF 316.

On what the airport earns from running an airport, after expenses, coverage is 1.20 in 2025 and between 0.98 and 1.06 in the five years after it. In 2026 and 2028 operations fall just short of the debt payment. In 2026 the airport earns $83.0 million from operations against a payment of $84.7 million.

Adding the designated revenue raises coverage to 1.12 in 2026. On that measure 5 of the 6 forecast years fall below 1.25. The rest is closed by the Coverage Account, a reserve the Authority may fund monthly from its own revenue, ninth in the indenture’s order of payment and capped at 25% of that year’s debt service. By this reporting’s calculation the forecast assumes the cap in five of the six years, and 23.23% in 2027.

What has been designated

Gas royalty was designated once, $3.8 million in 2019, and at zero in every year since and every year forecast through 2030.

From 2020 through 2022 the designated money was federal pandemic relief: $13.1 million, $19.1 million and $13.1 million. For 2023 the same statement prints two figures, $4.0 million in Exhibit E and $3.0 million in its management discussion, and this reporting chooses neither. In 2024 the Authority designated nothing.

From 2025 the designated block is slot-machine tax under the state Gaming Act. The Authority reported receiving $12.4 million a year under that act from 2020 through 2024, in years it designated none of it into the pledge.

In connection with a January 2025 majority-in-interest vote, the Authority committed to using discretionary revenue, “which may include Gaming Revenues or Natural Gas Revenues,” of at least $8.8 million in 2025 and $11.575 million a year for 2026 through 2028, to reduce airline rates and charges. Exhibit E carries $11,575,000 into 2029 and 2030, two years past the commitment. PDF 67.

The charge per boarded passenger

Interest on the 2021 and 2023 bonds was capitalized, which means it was paid out of the borrowing rather than out of operating revenue, through Feb. 1 and April 1 of 2025. Interest on the 2025 bonds was capitalized through Oct. 1 of that year, except for the portion supported by passenger facility charges, which the statement says is not capitalized. PDF 39.

Through those dates, one line in the calculation the airlines are billed against read zero. Also missing from the audited rate base in 2021, 2022, 2023 and 2024, every year the terminal was under construction: any debt service on it.

The audited charge fell to $7.34 per boarded passenger in 2024, the lowest of the ten years in that table. It had been $12.89 in 2015 and $20.50 in the pandemic year of 2020. Table IV, ACAA annual report 2024.

The consultant’s forecast is a different measure and belongs to a different table. On it the charge runs $15.71 in 2025, $19.13 in 2026, $19.44 in 2027, $19.06 in 2028, $20.25 in 2029 and $20.53 in 2030. Forecast boardings rise in every one of those years.

Four different figures are called cost per enplaned passenger: the audited annual report, the bond statement’s management discussion, the consultant’s forecast, and FAA Form 5100-127. For 2024 the first two read $7.34 and $11.56. Nothing here subtracts one from another.

Who is billed

The Authority credits its non-airline revenue and any revenue it designates against the cost of running the airport, and the airlines are billed the difference.

What is disputed

Whether a charge to an airline reaches a passenger is contested. The figure is an airline cost rather than a fare: it is the airport’s costs divided by the number of passengers the carriers board. What an airline does with that cost is a separate question.

The published research measures adjacent questions and none of it examines Pittsburgh. Koopmans and Lieshout, in the Journal of Air Transport Management in 2016, found an airline operating cost passed through at under 50% when it falls on one carrier and over 50% when it falls on the whole sector. Gayle and Lin, studying US domestic fuel costs from 2013 to 2015, estimated an elasticity of 0.37. Wozny, examining a Swedish ticket tax in 2018, found over-shifting in competitive markets and 0.21 in monopoly markets, and a ticket tax is not an airline operating cost.

What is not established

The Authority has not been asked to respond to any of this. No passenger and no taxpayer has been interviewed.

The Authority filed a 2026 charge of $19.13 with its investors in April 2025. It later gave the Post-Gazette a figure of $17.64. This reporting has not matched that second figure to any document, and the date it was given is unsettled. What the airlines are actually billed in 2026 has not been published.
