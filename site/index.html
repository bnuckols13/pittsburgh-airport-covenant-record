Pittsburgh airport's charge to airlines is higher than at 16 larger hubs, and the money that held it down is gone

Dek. The Allegheny County Airport Authority's own consultant expects the charge to reach $20.53 a passenger by 2030. On the revenue actually pledged to the terminal's bonds, the consultant's forecast comes in under the 1.25 coverage figure the Authority promised its lenders in eleven of fourteen forecast years. Two discretionary decisions close that gap, and the Authority makes both. It tops up the pledged revenue with money it designates each year, which from 2020 through 2023 was federal pandemic aid and is forecast from 2025 to be casino money it has not designated since 2019. It then clears the test with a second account it funds at its own option, filled to the legal ceiling in twelve of those fourteen years.

Airlines are charged $16.10 for every passenger they board at Pittsburgh International, more than they pay at Atlanta, Charlotte, Denver, Dallas-Fort Worth, Detroit, Minneapolis, Phoenix, Las Vegas, Orlando, Tampa, Nashville, Baltimore, Dulles, Reagan National, Fort Lauderdale or Houston Intercontinental. All 16 are large hubs. Atlanta boards 53 million passengers a year at $4.48.

Pittsburgh's charge was $10.35 in the 2019 budget. The Authority's consultant expects it to reach $20.53 by 2030.

Pittsburgh sets its airline rates on a residual basis, which means every other source of money the airport takes in is counted first and the carriers are billed the remainder of what the Authority needs that year for operating costs, debt payments and reserves. Borrowing for the new terminal enters that calculation directly, and the payments on it reach $111.3 million a year from 2027.

In September 2017 the board of the Authority voted to build a new landside terminal rather than renovate the one it had. Christina Cassotis, the Authority's chief executive, said that day that renovating "is actually not cheaper and we looked at it."

Federal guidance for airport master plans requires that comparison, weighing each option against "a wide range of evaluation criteria, including its operational, environmental, and financial impacts." The Authority's 2017 annual report says the plan is on file with the Federal Aviation Administration. Neither the Authority nor the FAA has released it, and no estimate for renovating the old terminal has appeared in the nine years since.

The Post-Gazette reported in October 2018, three years before the first bonds were sold, that the airline discounts "have been subsidized in part by oil and gas revenues from drilling on airport land and state gambling funds." The Authority named both sources itself at the groundbreaking.

In February 2018, four months after the board approved the project, John Fiorita, who had run US Airways' properties and facilities in Pittsburgh, asked the Post-Gazette whether the claimed savings had counted the maintenance still owed on the buildings the Authority was keeping. "Have any of those numbers been taken into account?" he said. In the same account a resident named David Allinder asked Allegheny County Council to hold a public hearing.

Council did not hold one. Its president said the county had no jurisdiction over the Authority. No elected body in Allegheny County has taken the question up since.

Council does confirm every member of the airport board. On July 8 it seated state Sen. Devlin Robinson, R-37th, 12-3. Bethany Hallam, Lissa Geiger Shulman and Alex Rose voted no. Robinson's office lists him as vice chair of the Senate Transportation Committee and a member of Appropriations, the committees that handle the state money the airport receives. None of the three who voted against him has explained the vote publicly.

David Minnotte, who chaired the board through the 2017 approval, left in April 2024 after 20 years. In January the board voted unanimously to extend Cassotis's contract through Jan. 31, 2030, past the year the airline contracts expire.

How secure the money is

The terminal is supposed to pay for itself out of what the airport earns, with a margin on top. That margin is the one commitment the financing has to satisfy every year, and it is written as a ratio: earnings of at least 1.25 times the annual debt payment.

The projection filed with investors in April 2025 runs six years. The document prints one combined figure for each year, and every one of them clears. It clears because the printed figure counts a second account beside the earnings.

Separate the two and the picture changes. Analyzed by this newsroom from the consultant's own printed rows, the airport's pledged earnings alone cover the debt payment 1.12 times in 2026, 1.13 in 2027, 1.12 in 2028, 1.18 in 2029 and 1.19 in 2030. Across the 2021 and 2025 statements together, fourteen forecast years, eleven come in under 1.25 on earnings alone. The shortfall the second account has to cover runs from $5.6 million to $11.7 million a year in the 2025 forecast, against $0.5 million to $5.1 million in the 2021 one.

That second account is the Coverage Account, and it sits ninth in the order in which the airport's money is spent. The indenture lets it count toward the test, so on the covenant as written the forecasts comply and no payment is missed in any forecast year. It is filled from the airport's own revenue, monthly, "at the discretion of the Authority," in an amount the Authority determines, up to a ceiling of 25 percent of the annual debt payment. In twelve of those fourteen forecast years the consultant assumes it is filled to that ceiling.

The margin behind a $1.7 billion building is a deposit the Authority decides on each month, and may decide against.

The money on the other side of the line

There is a second discretionary lever, and it works on the earnings rather than on the account.

Pledged earnings are not only what the airport takes in. The indenture lets the Authority designate outside money into them, under a category it calls Other Pledged Revenues, defined as "moneys, not constituting Revenues, that are designated, for any period" into the pledge. Those designations are counted inside the Net Revenues figure the covenant tests. They are also, like the Coverage Account, the Authority's own annual choice.

The Authority's itemization of what it has designated, at Exhibit E of the 2025 statement, does not show what the public record has assumed it shows.

From 2020 through 2023, the money designated into the pledge was federal pandemic aid. In 2021, the peak year at $19.1 million, every dollar came from CARES, CRRSA and ARPA relief. In 2022 the total was $13.1 million and again all federal. In 2023 it was $4.0 million of concession grants, federal as well. In 2024 the Authority designated nothing at all.

Across those five years it designated no gas royalties and no casino money. The last year in which it designated either was 2019, when $3.8 million of gas revenue and $1.7 million of gaming revenue went into the pledge. Since then, on the Authority's own exhibit, both lines read zero.

The forecast has them return. Beginning in 2025 the consultant assumes $8.2 million a year in discretionary gaming revenue, rising to $8.5 million, alongside a supplemental gaming contribution for the baggage handling system that starts at $630,000 and reaches $3.5 million by 2030. From 2025 those two lines are the entire designation, and they are inside the same Net Revenues figure that comes in under 1.25 in eleven of fourteen years.

That money exists. Gaming has been flat at $12.4 million a year since 2020, under a statute the Authority's own filing says could be amended "to reduce or eliminate payments." Gas royalties swung between $5.6 million and $12.2 million over six years, and in May 2022 the Authority amended its drilling lease to let CNX deduct costs from gross revenue on new wells, a change the filing says "resulted in a reduction in the total percentage of revenues received by the Authority." Neither source is promised to the bondholders. Neither has been designated into the pledge in five years. The forecast turns both back on.

The same statement gives two different figures for what was designated in 2023, $3.0 million in its management discussion and $4.0 million in the consultant's exhibit, and the discrepancy has not been explained.

What it costs to fly out of Pittsburgh

When the airport needs more money than it earns, the airlines are billed the difference under their contracts.

Carriers are not charged per passenger. They pay rent and landing fees, $298.36 per square foot of terminal space and $4.06 per 1,000 pounds of landing weight as of January. The per-passenger figure is that total divided by boardings. "It is not a ticket fee charged to passengers," Bob Kerlik, the Authority's spokesman, wrote in the airport's own publication in 2019, "but rather the total of all airport costs to airlines divided by the carriers' number of departing passengers."

KBRA, rating the 2025 bonds, listed among the credit challenges that "a rising CPE could discourage service expansion or retention, particularly for LCCs and ULCCs." Low-cost and ultra-low-cost carriers flew 29.7 percent of Pittsburgh's departing passengers in 2014 and 44.3 percent in 2024. Seth Lehman, a senior director at Fitch Ratings, told the Bond Buyer in April 2025 that the borrowing would push Pittsburgh's cost "above what is standard for an A-rated airport."

Studies of per-passenger taxes added at booking find carriers passing along nearly all of the cost. Studies of operating costs find less. Thijs Koopmans and Rogier Lieshout, in the Journal of Air Transport Management in 2016, put a cost increase that hits one carrier below 50 percent and one that hits a whole sector above it. None of that work examines Pittsburgh.

Passengers pay the airport directly as well. A $4.50 charge sits on every eligible ticket and did not come down when the terminal opened, because federal law caps it at $4.50 for nearly every airport in the country. Rental customers have paid $8 a day since Jan. 1, 2025. Pennsylvania taxpayers pay through a $12.4 million appropriation of gambling money that goes to the airport instead of somewhere else.

The passengers came

The obvious version of this story is that a public authority inflated its passenger forecast to justify a building. The record does not support it.

The 2021 forecast projected 4,924,000 boardings for 2024 if traffic recovered strongly and 4,369,000 if it recovered slowly. The year came in at 4,964,361, which beat the strong case and cleared the slow one by nearly 600,000. In 2025 passengers again beat the base case, and the charge landed three cents above the airport's own worst case.

The forecast that was revised is not the one that was wrong. Between its 2021 and 2023 bond statements the Authority carried the passenger forecast forward unchanged and raised the forecast cost 23.6 percent.

What they said it would cost

Between 2017 and 2021, three officials of the Authority told the public what airlines would pay once the new terminal opened.

Cassotis said the charge would fall from $12.69 to $9.73 by 2023. Kerlik said the project would use "no local tax dollars" and had airline support "because it will lower costs." At the groundbreaking in October 2021, Chief Financial Officer Eric Sprys said the cost should end up between $10 and $11.

The charge was $11.34 in 2023, the year Cassotis had said it would be $9.73. The same statement prints $11.50 for that year on a different page. It is $16.10 now. It was $14.97 in 2011, before the gas and gambling money was ever spent on it.

Patrick Bowes, then American Airlines' representative in Pittsburgh, said of the $9.73: "We feel very confident that we'll get there."

The document the Authority filed with investors eight weeks before Sprys spoke put the opening year at $12.92 in the good case and $16.07 in the bad one.

Two figures for this year do not agree. The statement filed for investors in April 2025 forecasts $19.13. The Authority told the Post-Gazette in January it expects $17.64. A budget adopted later can legitimately differ from a forecast made earlier. The number given to lenders is the higher one.

The comparison nobody has seen

The savings that justified the project were the maintenance the Authority would stop paying once it closed the old terminal, which is the arithmetic the Allegheny Institute went at three weeks after the vote, setting roughly $23 million a year of avoided upkeep against roughly $75 million a year of debt service on what was then a $1.1 billion project, a comparison that has since moved the wrong way twice over, because the project is $1.7 billion now and the annual payment reaches $111.3 million from 2027, while the maintenance saving has never been demonstrated.

The old building is closed and still standing. The line for demolishing it read $33,000,000 in the bond statement the Authority sold in 2023 and $0 in the one it sold in April 2025. The statement says the Authority "postponed the demolition of the existing landside building terminal to reduce project costs and associated debt," and puts the cost of that postponement at $2,047,000 a year in additional operating expense, which the signatory airlines agreed to.

Kansas City opened a new single terminal in February 2023, in a market of about the same size with no hub carrier feeding it. Its charge to airlines for fiscal 2025 is $15.37, and its own bond documents forecast that figure peaking and then coming down to $16.68 by 2027. Pittsburgh's climbs every year through 2030.

Tasso Katselas designed the terminal Pittsburgh replaced. Weeks before the first bonds were sold he told the Post-Gazette: "There's no way anyone can justify it that has any sense."

Three things expire on Dec. 31, 2028: the contracts that make the airlines responsible for whatever the airport cannot earn, the commitment of gas and gambling money, and the waiver on the $5 million the Authority would otherwise deposit each year into its own capital fund. The bonds run to 2056.

A copy of the master plan is on file with the FAA.
